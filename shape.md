# Causal Shape Framework

*Revised November 20, 2026*

“Terrain is the stage. Flow is what happens over time. There are actors, too.”

Use the framework to read where a situation is likely to go if it continues, and to find the fork where a choice could bring you closer to what you want. Being able to say “from A, B looks plausible and D looks unlikely” can be enough.

## Set the goal and draw the course

First decide what you want to change and which stretch of the flow to examine. Choose the time span that suits the question. Always examine a pair. In a conversation, that might be you and the other person. For water, it is the sending side and the receiving side.

The stage has sources, paths, forks, junctions, return paths and endpoints. A source is an inlet with continuing replenishment from outside. Flow can also begin from a reservoir with no source. A glass that already contains water can pour some out.

Four shapes describe what the flow does over time. The conditions determine their order.

| Shape | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Runs out | Replenishment falls behind. The difference driving the flow disappears and the flow comes to a stop. |
| Strengthens | The result of movement feeds back and widens the difference driving the next movement. |
| Keeps flowing | Replenishment maintains the difference, so movement continues. |
| Smolders | A difference exists, but a closed valve blocks movement along that path. |

An actor can **steer** by choosing a path or braking, **change the terrain** by altering a path or its conditions, or **communicate** to influence the other actor. The other actor has a steering wheel of their own.

## Five parts

| Part | What to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Difference | The gap between comparable levels or strengths on sides A and B. |
| Resistance | Something that reduces flow throughout its passage. |
| Reservoir | A place that holds a quantity, along a path or at its endpoint. |
| Valve | Something that closes a path until a condition is met. |
| Momentum | Movement carried over from before. It has a direction and can sustain movement even after the difference disappears. |

Use these terms. Leave anything that has no name yet unnamed.

## Read a situation

With the goal in mind, ask these questions in order.

1. Who or what is on the other side of this pair?
2. Where is the source, if there is one?
3. Where is the reservoir?
4. Is there a fork?
5. Is there a return path?
6. What is blocking the way?
7. Could the observer's point of view be biasing the reading?

If you can see the fork but cannot yet choose a direction, ask what you could observe that would help you decide.

## When to calculate

Stop when a directional reading answers the question. A rough 0–3 rating can help describe the size of an observation. Calculate a transfer only when the same quantity moves from A to B and both levels can be compared on a common scale.

A closed valve permits zero transfer. With the valve open, use the difference divided by resistance to estimate the amount driven by the difference. Cap that contribution at the amount that would bring the two levels even. Then account for momentum in its own direction. Limit the actual transfer to what the sending side holds and what the receiving side has room for. Subtract the amount transferred from the sender, add it to the receiver, and read the difference again.

For example, take two vessels of equal, constant cross-sectional area, with room to receive the water. A starts at 2 and B at 0. With the valve open and zero momentum, transferring 1 gives A = 1 and B = 1. The levels are even and the flow stops there.
